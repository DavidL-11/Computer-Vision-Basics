SVD visualized

The singular value decomposition \(A = U \Sigma V^\top\) splits every matrix into a rotation, a scaling along the axes and another rotation. Watch a 2 × 2 matrix turn the unit circle into an ellipse step by step, solve \(Ax = b\) with the pseudo-inverse, and rebuild an image from only its largest singular values.

The singular value decomposition

Every real \(m \times n\) matrix \(A\) can be written as

$$ A = U \Sigma V^\top, \qquad U^\top U = V V^\top = I $$
  • \(U\) (\(m \times m\)) is orthogonal; its columns \(u_i\) are the left singular vectors,
  • \(\Sigma\) (\(m \times n\)) is diagonal with the singular values \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0\),
  • \(V\) (\(n \times n\)) is orthogonal; its columns \(v_i\) are the right singular vectors.

The singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of \(A^\top A\) (and of \(A A^\top\)), and the \(v_i\) are the eigenvectors of \(A^\top A\). The value panel checks \(\sigma_i^2 = \lambda_i\) for the current matrix. The larger a singular value, the more its pair \(u_i, v_i\) contributes to \(A\).

Rotate, scale, rotate

Read \(A x = U\,(\Sigma\,(V^\top x))\) from right to left. \(V^\top\) is orthogonal, so it only turns the plane: the unit circle stays a circle, and \(v_1, v_2\) land on the axes \(e_1, e_2\). \(\Sigma\) stretches the axes by \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\): the circle becomes an axis-aligned ellipse. \(U\) turns this ellipse into place. So \(A\) maps the unit circle to an ellipse with semi-axes \(\sigma_1 u_1\) and \(\sigma_2 u_2\), and

$$ A v_i = \sigma_i u_i $$

The panels use the usual math orientation, \(x\) right and \(y\) up. Singular vectors are only defined up to sign: flipping \(v_i\) and \(u_i\) together gives the same \(A\). Here \(V\) is always chosen as a pure rotation. When \(\det A < 0\), \(A\) flips the plane, and then \(U\) contains a mirror: the small arrow on the circle runs clockwise in the last panel. \(|\det A| = \sigma_1 \sigma_2\) is the area of the ellipse divided by \(\pi\).

Solving \(Ax = b\) with the pseudo-inverse

Undoing the three steps in reverse order gives the solution: rotate \(b\) back with \(U^\top\), divide by the singular values, rotate with \(V\):

$$ Ax = b \;\Rightarrow\; x = V \Sigma^{-1} U^\top b $$

When a singular value is tiny, dividing by it blows up every error in \(b\) along \(u_i\). When it is zero, \(A\) squashes the plane onto a line, most \(b\) are not reachable, and the division is impossible. The pseudo-inverse replaces \(1/\sigma_i\) by 0 for all \(\sigma_i \le \tau\). The result is the least-squares solution, the \(x\) that minimizes \(|Ax - b|\), and among all of those the shortest one. In the last panel, the ring \(Ax\) then sits at the point of the line closest to \(b\). This works for any shape of \(A\), including overdetermined systems with more equations than unknowns.

Rank-\(p\) approximation

Written as a sum, the SVD builds \(A\) from rank-1 layers \(\sigma_k u_k v_k^\top\), one per singular value. Keeping only the first \(p\) of them gives

$$ a_{ij} \approx \sum_{k=1}^{p} u_{ik} \, \sigma_k \, v_{jk} $$

It is the best approximation of rank \(p\): no other matrix of rank \(p\) is closer to \(A\) in the sum of squared differences, and that sum is exactly \(\sum_{k > p} \sigma_k^2\). A grayscale image is an \(m \times n\) matrix, so it can be stored as \(p\) columns of \(U\) and \(V\) plus \(p\) singular values, \(p\,(m + n + 1)\) numbers instead of \(m n\). This is the dimensionality reduction from the lecture: the first singular vectors capture the dominant structure, the last ones mostly fine detail and noise.

Where it comes back

The same decomposition solves the homogeneous problem \(Ax = 0\): among all unit vectors, \(x = v_n\), the right singular vector of the smallest singular value, makes \(|Ax|\) smallest, and \(|A v_n| = \sigma_n\). This is the tool behind total least squares, estimating a homography and calibrating a camera.

Try this

  • Choose Rotation: \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1\), the circle stays a circle and \(\Sigma = I\). Then choose Scaling: now \(U = V = I\) and only the middle step does anything.
  • Choose Shear, \([1\ 1;\ 0\ 1]\): its singular values are \(1.618\) and \(0.618\), the golden ratio and its inverse. Their product is \(\det A = 1\): a shear keeps the area.
  • Choose Mirror: \(\det A = -1\), the last step reads "rotate + mirror" and the small arrow on the ellipse turns clockwise.
  • Choose Nearly singular and set \(\tau = 0\). Click \(b\) a little off the long axis of the ellipse: \(1/\sigma_2 \approx 50\) sends \(x\) far outside the first panel. Raise \(\tau\) above \(\sigma_2 \approx 0.02\): \(x\) jumps back to a short vector, and \(Ax\) (ring) moves onto the long axis, no longer exactly on \(b\).
  • Choose Singular: the ellipse collapses to a segment and \(\sigma_2 = 0\). Wherever you click \(b\), the ring \(Ax\) lands at the closest point of the segment's line, and \(x\) stays on the line through \(\pm v_1\).
  • Set \(p = 1\): the image consists of copies of one row, each scaled by a different factor. Increase \(p\) and watch the PSNR rise. Above \(p = 68\), \(p\,(m + n + 1)\) exceeds \(m n = 19200\) and storing the SVD costs more than the image.
  • Compare the images: the test chart, made of axis-aligned blocks, has singular values that drop steeply, so a small \(p\) already looks sharp. The zone plate's circles need many more.
